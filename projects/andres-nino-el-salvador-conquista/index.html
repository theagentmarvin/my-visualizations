Andrés Niño's Journey and Role in El Salvador during the Spanish Conquest

Early 1520s — The First Documented European Exploration

Research Report 2026

Executive Summary

Andrés Niño, a Spanish pilot-major, led the first documented European exploration of El Salvador's Pacific coast in 1522. Sailing northwest from Panama with Gil González Dávila's expedition, Niño discovered the Gulf of Fonseca, landed on Meanguera Island (Petronila), explored Jiquilisco Bay, and sighted the Lempa River mouth.

His voyage marked the "discovery" of Salvadoran territory but involved no conquest—actual subjugation began in 1524 under Pedro de Alvarado against Pipil strongholds like Cuzcatlán. Niño's role was navigational and exploratory, amid rival Spanish advances from Mexico and Panama. He perished around 1525 in Honduras.

Sources: Chronicles (Oviedo), modern histories. [1][2]

Timeline

January 1522

Niño joins Gil González Dávila's fleet (3 caravels) from Panamá to Nicoya Peninsula (Costa Rica). Niño pilots ahead northwest. [3]

May 31, 1522

Lands on Meanguera Island (Gulf of Fonseca), names it Petronila—first Spanish footfall in Salvadoran territory. [4]

1522

Charts Fonseca Gulf (names it after Juan Rodríguez de Fonseca), Jiquilisco Bay, Lempa River mouth; sails to Tehuantepec Gulf (Mexico). [5]

~1525

Niño dies inland in Honduras during further expeditions. [6]

Key Figures

Andrés Niño

Pilot-major; expert navigator from Santo Domingo. Discovered key coastal features; no military conquest role. [7]

Gil González Dávila

Expedition captain; missionary-conquistador from the Nicaragua push. [8]

Pedro de Alvarado

Led the 1524 invasion from Guatemala; battled the Pipil at Acajutla and Tacuzcalco. [9]

Indigenous Peoples

Pipil lords (Cuzcatlán); Lenca in the east. Smallpox (1520 onward) had weakened them before contact. [10]

Historical Context

Pre-1522: Indigenous El Salvador

Before European arrival, El Salvador (known as Kuskatan or Cuzcatlán) was home to:

  • Pipil Nahua — Central-west region, cacao-rich
  • Lenca — Eastern territories
  • Ch'orti'/Poqomam Maya — Northern areas
  • Xinca/Mangue — Southern regions

Population estimates range from 130,000 to 1 million. Fragmented polities traded cacao and obsidian. [11]

Spanish Expansion Dynamics

Following Cortés's conquest of Mexico (1521), rival expeditions pushed from:

  • Panamá — Led by Pedrarias Dávila
  • Guatemala — Led by Alvarado

Niño's coastal survey bridged the Panama-Nicaragua-Guatemala claims. No gold was found; the focus shifted to land and labor (encomienda system). The actual conquest (1524-1539) was violent: battles, uprisings (including the Atlacatl resistance), and diseases halved the native population. [12]
