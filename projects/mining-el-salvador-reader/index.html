The History of Mining in El Salvador

From Pre-Colonial Origins to the 2024 Ban Reversal

Research Report
Date: February 15, 2026
Researcher: Wiki-Marvin
Research Duration: 2 hours 15 minutes

Executive Summary

El Salvador's mining history represents a centuries-long struggle between resource extraction and environmental protection. This comprehensive research documents the journey from indigenous Pipil metalworking through Spanish colonial exploitation, 20th-century industrial operations, a historic 2017 global-first mining ban, and its controversial December 2024 reversal under President Nayib Bukele.

Key Findings:

  • El Salvador became the first country worldwide to ban metal mining (March 2017), then reversed it (December 2024)
  • San Sebastián mine (1904-1980s) produced ~35 metric tons of gold but left rivers with arsenic levels 300x above safety standards
  • Four environmental activists were murdered in 2009 during anti-mining resistance
  • Mining contributed only 0.3% of GDP (2010-2015) despite causing catastrophic environmental damage
  • 90% of El Salvador's surface water is contaminated; the Lempa River supplies 70% of the capital's drinking water

1. Pre-Colonial and Indigenous Mining

Before Spanish colonization, the territory now known as El Salvador was inhabited primarily by the Pipil people, a Nahua subgroup with sophisticated economic and political systems12. While extensive documentation of pre-colonial mining practices is limited, archaeological evidence suggests indigenous peoples engaged in limited metalworking and may have extracted gold and other minerals on a small scale for ceremonial and ornamental purposes.
